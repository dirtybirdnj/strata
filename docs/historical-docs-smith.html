William Smith

23 March 1769 — 28 August 1839

Father of English Geology • Creator of the First Geological Map of a Nation

strata (core package)

The Founding Principle

"The same strata were found always in the same order of superposition and contained the same peculiar fossils. Organized fossils are to the naturalist as coins to the antiquary; they are the antiquities of the earth." — William Smith, 1816

William Smith was a self-taught surveyor who revolutionized our understanding of the Earth. Working on the Somerset Coal Canal in the 1790s, he observed something no geologist had noticed before: rock layers (strata) always appeared in the same order, and each layer contained distinctive fossils. This insight—the principle of faunal succession—became the foundation of modern geology and stratigraphy.

Smith was not a gentleman scientist. He was a working man who spent his life in coal mines, canal cuttings, and quarries. While academics debated theories in comfortable studies, Smith walked the land, chipped at rock faces with his hammer, and recorded what he saw. Over twenty years, he traveled over 10,000 miles on foot and horseback, mapping the geology of England and Wales.

In 1815, he published his masterwork: "A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales"—the first geological map of an entire nation. Each stratum was shown in a different color, arranged in proper superposition. The map was over eight feet tall and five feet wide, hand-colored, sold by subscription. It was a triumph of observation over theory, of patient fieldwork over armchair speculation.

This principle—that order matters, that layers must be properly superposed—applies with equal force to the construction of maps. The Strata package bears his name because it embodies his philosophy: layer order is everything.

Biographical Timeline

1769 — Born in Churchill, Oxfordshire; father was the village blacksmith; orphaned at age eight
1787 — Apprenticed to Edward Webb, land surveyor, in Stow-on-the-Wold; learned surveying, measurement, and observation
1791 — First professional survey in Oxfordshire; began noticing patterns in rock formations
1793-1794 — Employed to survey Somerset Coal Canal; in the canal cuttings discovered principle of faunal succession—rocks always appeared in same order with same fossils
1795 — Purchased estate at Tucking Mill near Bath; began systematic collection of fossils and rocks
1799 — June 11: Dictates first "Order of the Strata" at the Swan Inn, Dunkerton—23 named strata in proper sequence, witnessed and dated
1801 — Prepared first geological map of area around Bath; hand-colored manuscript
1804 — Prepared prospectus for national geological map; began collecting subscriptions
1815 — August: Published "A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales with part of Scotland"—first geological map of a nation; 8'6" × 5'1", hand-colored, sold for five guineas
1816-1819 — Published "Strata Identified by Organized Fossils" with hand-colored plates of characteristic fossils
1819 — Financial ruin; imprisoned for debt in King's Bench Prison for ten weeks; forced to sell Tucking Mill and his fossil collection (bought by British Museum for £700)
1820-1824 — Wandered England as itinerant surveyor; produced county maps of Yorkshire
1828 — Settled in Scarborough; designed the Rotunda Museum—circular building displaying geology in stratigraphic order
1831 — Received first Wollaston Medal from Geological Society of London; Society president Adam Sedgwick named him "the Father of English Geology"
1832 — Granted government pension of £100/year in recognition of his contributions
1839 — Died in Northampton on way to Birmingham meeting; buried at St Peter's Church, Northampton

The Great Map

Smith's 1815 map—formally titled "A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales with part of Scotland"—was revolutionary in its conception and execution. At over eight feet tall, it showed the entire nation with each geological stratum painted in a distinctive color, graduated to show the direction of dip (darker at the base, lighter toward the top).

"I determined to construct a map of this kind myself, and have been twenty years employed in so doing." — William Smith, 1815

The map was not merely beautiful—it was predictive. Using the principle of superposition, Smith could tell miners where to dig for coal, engineers where to find building stone, and farmers what kind of soil to expect. The theoretical elegance of his discovery had immediate practical application.

But success brought competition. The Geological Society of London—a gentleman's club that had denied Smith membership—published a competing map in 1820, borrowing heavily from Smith's work without credit. Unable to compete with their resources, Smith's business failed, and he was imprisoned for debt. Only in 1831 did the Society acknowledge his priority, awarding him the first Wollaston Medal and naming him "the Father of English Geology."

Connection to Strata

William Smith embodies the core principle of the Strata package: layer order matters. Just as Smith discovered that rock layers appear in a consistent order determined by geological processes, our cartographic layers must be ordered correctly.

Layer Ordering in Strata

Background Layer (Bottom)

The lowest stratum—like bedrock, it provides the foundation. Typically a solid color or paper texture.

- name: background # First in list = bottom layer

Water Bodies

Above the background, water features—lakes, rivers, oceans—define the liquid landscape.

- name: lake_champlain # Water cuts through land

Land Areas

Municipalities, counties, states—with water subtracted to show only the land portion.

- name: towns
subtract: lake_champlain

Linear Features (Top)

Roads, borders, rivers—line features that must remain visible above filled areas.

- name: roads # Last in list = top layer

The package name "Strata" directly honors Smith's fundamental insight: understanding comes from recognizing and respecting the natural ordering of layers. Put water below land and it disappears. Put roads below polygons and they're hidden. The order is not arbitrary—it is determined by the nature of the data, just as the order of rock strata is determined by the processes that formed them.
